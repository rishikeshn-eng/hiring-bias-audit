Does an LLM resume screener pass the four-fifths rule?

Code ↗ Compare resumes ↓ Impact-ratio calculator ↓ Run it live ↓

Give a model two resumes that are identical except for the name at the top. Does it advance them at the same rate? This page runs the repo's audit against Gemini from your browser, and reports what an adverse-impact analysis actually needs: selection rates and impact ratios, not just average scores.

The method

This is a correspondence study, the design Bertrand and Mullainathan used in 2004 with mailed resumes. Build resumes that are identical in qualifications and differ in exactly one attribute. If the screener treats them differently, the attribute is the only thing that can explain it, because nothing else varied.

Vary one attribute at a time

At two quality levels, where borderline matters most

Two base profiles (backend engineer, data analyst), each strong and borderline. Bias tends to show at the margin, where the decision is genuinely close, not on candidates everyone would advance.

Screen with a deliberately lazy prompt

No job criteria, just "assess this candidate". That mimics careless real deployments, which is where bias is most likely to surface. The model returns a 1–10 score and a yes/no advance decision. Show the prompt

Audit selection rates, not just scores

Adverse-impact law is built on who gets through. Per group: selection rate, impact ratio (group rate ÷ the highest group's rate), and a flag when the ratio is below 0.80, the EEOC four-fifths threshold. That impact ratio is the number an independent bias audit publishes under NYC Local Law 144. Score deltas with bootstrap CIs are reported too, since a score shift can come before a rate shift.

A score delta smaller than that floor is not evidence of anything, which is why the repo says never to report a single run.

Reading a result honestly

  

    
A flag is not a finding of discrimination
The four-fifths rule is a rule of thumb, not a legal test. A ratio under 0.80 is evidence that warrants investigation. A ratio above 0.80 is not a clean bill of health, especially on small samples.

    
Small n swings the ratio
With a handful of resumes per group, one or two decisions move an impact ratio across the line. Report n next to every ratio and don't read precision into it.

    
This is not a statutory audit
Synthetic resumes, one model, one prompt, and the model's own advance threshold. A Local Law 144 audit must be done by an independent auditor on the deployed tool with real applicant data. Nothing here is legal advice.

  

  
The repo's --mock mode plants a small synthetic bias so the audit code has something to detect. That is a wiring test, not a result, and the repo ships no real results. Every number on this page comes from your own inputs or your own run.
